# Data-Driven Adaptive Chirp Mode Decomposition and Its Application in Mechanical Fault Diagnosis under Non-stationary Conditions [with MATLAB Code]

Rotating machinery, such as wind turbines, aero engines, and train wheel pairs, occupy significant positions in modern industrial practices. The condition monitoring and fault diagnosis of these rotating machines are crucial for ensuring their safe and stable operation [1-7]. Due to complex operating environments, the condition monitoring signals exhibit non-stationary amplitude modulation and frequency modulation (AM-FM) characteristics, commonly referred to as nonlinear chirp signals (NCS) [8]. Real NCS typically contain many non-stationary sub-signals (referred to as linear frequency modulation modes), each of which usually contains valuable information related to the machine's health status [9, 10]. To effectively implement condition monitoring and fault diagnosis, it is necessary to decompose the signals into a series of independent chirp modes [11].
Over the past few decades, researchers have developed numerous methods to decompose complex non-stationary signals. The Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD) was proposed by Huang et al. [12]. It employs a recursive filtering algorithm based on local extrema information to extract signal patterns, demonstrating strong adaptability and efficiency. However, EMD is affected by issues such as mode mixing, boundary effects, and sensitivity to noise and sampling [13, 14]. To address these problems, several improved versions of EMD were developed, including Integrated EMD (EEMD) [15] and Time-Varying Filtered EMD (TVF-EMD) [16]. These methods, however, only address certain issues and still do not meet the requirements of some complex application scenarios [17, 18].
Due to the fact that most signals often have a certain frequency range, many methods separate signal patterns by constructing filters in the frequency domain. Based on the concept of adaptive decomposition and wavelet transform theory, empirical wavelet transform (EWT) adaptively decomposes the signal spectrum into a series of continuous bands, then constructs a bandpass filter to extract signal patterns with compact support spectra [21]. However, it is difficult to accurately determine the frequency band boundaries for each pattern in strong noise environments. Variational Mode Decomposition (VMD) is another frequency domain filtering method that simultaneously obtains all signal patterns through joint optimization schemes [22]. VMD is theoretically grounded and can adaptively estimate the center frequencies of each mode in noise environments [23]. However, the core assumption of VMD is that signal patterns are narrowband and separated in the frequency domain. Therefore, this method is not suitable for the extraction of broadband non-stationary signals, especially when the spectral overlap of modes occurs [24].
To effectively analyze broadband non-stationary signals, the Variational Nonlinear Linear Frequency Modulation Decomposition (VNCMD) is proposed based on optimal demodulation techniques [25]. This method not only exhibits good convergence performance in noisy environments but also effectively decomposes linear frequency modulated signals that are very close or even cross each other. However, similar to VMD, VNCMD requires predefined signal mode numbers and bandwidth parameters [26] which are difficult to determine in practical applications. To overcome this issue, an Adaptive Linear Frequency Modulation Mode Decomposition (ACMD) is further proposed based on the Matching Pursuit framework [27]. This method extracts signal modes one by one through a recursive scheme to learn the number of signal modes. Due to its good adaptability and higher time-frequency resolution, ACMD is widely applied in many fields [28—30]. However, both VNCMD and ACMD estimate IF and mode iteratively using optimization algorithms, hence the algorithm's convergence performance largely depends on the initial IF. Most methods assume that IF can be extracted with acceptable accuracy by detecting time-frequency representation (TFR) ridges [31, 32]. However, the resolution of TFR is limited by the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle, resulting in limited precision of IF estimation. Moreover, ridge detection methods search for extrema within a given range, meaning it is challenging to extract near-mode IF. It is worth noting that ACMD reconstructs IF through a Hilbert transform-based method, which is only applicable to signals with different energy levels [33].
As mentioned above, most data-driven methods (such as EMD and LMD) have high adaptability but poor noise robustness. In contrast, methods with stronger noise robustness (such as VMD, VNCMD, and ACMD) are often subject to more conditions and prior information. In other words, most existing methods cannot simultaneously satisfy high adaptability and good noise robustness when analyzing non-stationary signals. In this article, the data-driven ACMD (DD-ACMD) method proposes a solution to the above problem. On one hand, it introduces a mid-frequency estimation method based on signal derivative normalization to estimate the mid-frequency of multi-component signals' highest frequency mode, breaking through the energy limitation of mid-frequency initialization in ACMD based on Hilbert transform; on the other hand, to reduce the impact of noise on mid-frequency estimation, an iterative time-varying filtering method based on demodulation technology is introduced to provide an initial mid-frequency for the ACMD iterative optimization algorithm. The proposed data-driven mid-frequency initialization method in DD-ACMD not only has high adaptability similar to EMD and LMD but also exhibits good noise robustness when analyzing complex non-stationary signals. Furthermore, a time-varying low-pass filter is introduced into the original recursive framework of ACMD to further improve the overall noise robustness of the entire algorithm.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, after a brief introduction to ACMD, a simulation example is provided to illustrate the limitations of the method. The proposed DD-ACMD method is detailed in Section 3. In Section 4, we present some simulation signals and actual signals to test the performance of DD-ACMD. We conclude our paper in Section 5.
